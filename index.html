The state of play

What August did to all twelve of you, deal by deal, and the haul table as declared.

The strangest offseason this league has ever had is over. Twenty–four keepers locked at the deadline, five trades landed in the final 72 hours, and one deal slid in at 9:41 pm with the league manager holding the door. Here is where it all landed.

Three dates you need

Keeper deadline: Saturday 29 August at 9:00 pm
Draft day: Sunday 6 September, 2pm — live on Zoom
In-season trade deadline: Wednesday 25 November, noon

The draft is settled: Sunday 6 September at 2pm, Labor Day weekend, with a Zoom running the whole way through. Camera on, sound on, and bring something to say — half the value of this league is the four hours you spend explaining your picks to eleven people who disagree.

Keepers locked at the deadline on the 29th — extended to 11 pm by the league manager so one last deal could clear. Pre–draft trading closes Sunday 30 August at 9 pm, and then the board is what it is: every keeper now travels with the pick he burned, per the rules below.

This was always going to be a strange year. The keeper rule that makes this league interesting — you can keep a man once, then he has to move or go back in the pool — meant that going into this offseason, ten of the twelve most valuable contracts in the league were held by owners who could not use them. Jaxon Smith–Njigba at an 8th. Ja'Marr Chase at a 5th. Jonathan Taylor at a 6th. CeeDee Lamb at a 6th. All of it dead in the hands of the men holding it — in May.

Then, over four days in late May, four owners figured out the answer at the same time: if my dead asset is your live one and yours is mine, we just swap. Nobody pays anybody. Both sides go from zero to a franchise keeper. It happened three times in seventy–two hours:

02 / The twelve owners

The book on everybody

Twelve characters, one card each: the story, the pair he locked, the grudge he cannot escape.

Sixteen years, 1,260 games, 2,776 draft picks, 143 trades and 895 waiver claims on record — run back through twelve men and what they did with them. Who each of you actually is when the record is laid out end to end, and where that record says you finish in 2026. That is the cast. The rest of this book is what they do about it.

Nobody gets the benefit of the doubt here and nobody gets buried. The archetype, the projection and every number underneath them come out of the same place: what you have actually done, every September since 2010. A man who says he drafts for value and then spends fourteen straight first rounds on running backs is a man who drafts running backs.

The player cards below introduce the characters in this epic, and chronicle sixteen years of feuds. Darryl Powell is The Champion — defending champion at 12–2, holding Jonathan Taylor and CeeDee Lamb, the best keeper pair ever declared here. Sean Quincey is The Five Timers Club — five rings, and 48 trades, more than anyone; when the phone rings, it is him. Kevin Flanagan is The Dynasty — three titles, a 16–15 blood feud with Brian Haas across 31 meetings, and the 1,777.8–point season that somehow won him nothing. Brian Haas is The Gambler — the worst draft record in the league and the second–biggest keeper haul in it, which tells you exactly how he plays. Dannie Walker is The Broker — two titles, four more finals lost, one of them to Sean Quincey by nine–tenths of a point.

Patrick Hanna is The Vault, because nobody has cracked it yet — sixteen seasons, zero titles, and Ja'Marr Chase at a 4th, the single best contract in the league. Kevin Clemens is The Metronome — the only owner whose keeper record is essentially flat; he wins it at the draft table or not at all. Brian Clemens is The Bridesmaid — a title so old it predates the ESPN records, and George Pickens at a 5th as his way back. Eric Counihan is The Faded Power — two early titles, none since 2013, and Darryl Powell has beaten him fourteen times in seventeen tries. Ken Tinkham is The Draft Savant — the best open–draft owner in league history, six trades in seven years, and not one final to show for any of it. James Lombardi is The Assassin — the 2024 title from ninth on the board that nobody saw coming, and this year the smallest keeper haul in the league, bought in the last 72 hours; he lives on the draft board. And Jon Nairn is The Understudy — 2–12 last year, years in the wings, then the August trade with Beans that took his keepers from dead last to seventh overnight. Every card below ends with the opponent its owner cannot afford to lose to — in this league you never play a stranger.

Pick a name below to read one at a time, or show all twelve. Cards are ordered by 2026 keeper haul, which is as close to a preseason power ranking as this league has. Each one carries an archetype, a projected finish with the reasoning attached, a scouting report on how he drafts, trades and bids, and the rivalries he cannot escape. Argue with any of it — but argue with the numbers, because that is all this is.

The rule this implies

Look for the swap first, every time. A trade between two owners who both hold dead assets is the only trade in this format where both sides genuinely win, and it costs neither of them a pick. Only when no swap exists should picks change hands — and then the going rate is high, because the buyer is bidding against ten other teams for a shrinking pool.

Marginal value: the number almost everyone gets wrong

You can only keep two players. So an incoming keeper is not worth his surplus — he is worth his surplus minus the man he pushes out of your top two. That is the whole explanation for deadline week. Zay Flowers at +142 was worth nearly all of it to James Lombardi, whose second–best declaration would otherwise have been a kicker at +47, and worth nothing at all to the teams above him. Rashee Rice at +215 was the best asset on the board and drew no buyer, because the four owners who could have used him were the four least willing to spend a pick.

This is why the same asset drew wildly different offers, and why the bottom of the keeper table did the buying while the top of it sat still. Remember it next August: your surplus is not the price, the gap is.

Keepers are due the Saturday, trades close the Sunday after, and the draft is the Sunday after that. If you are planning a deal, the date that binds you is 29 August, not draft day.

The lineup you are filling

Eight starters out of a fifteen–man roster: one quarterback, one back, two receivers, one tight end, one flex that takes a back or a receiver, a defence and a kicker, with seven on the bench and one injured–reserve slot on top. Twelve teams start twelve backs, twenty–four receivers and twelve flex spots between them.

That shape is why the pick chart looks the way it does. A league that starts only one back at a fixed slot makes the twentieth–best back roughly as valuable as the thirtieth–best receiver, and it makes a tight end who scores like a receiver genuinely scarce, because there are only twelve of those jobs and no flex to absorb the twelfth.

Money

$100 in. $600 champion, $250 runner–up, $100 third, $100 season high points, $100 Survivor, $50 playoff high points. Pay before the end of week one and you get 50 bonus points.

Keepers, in plain terms

  • Two per team, from last year's final roster, declared at the deadline.
  • Cost is the round he was drafted in. Keeping is free of penalty — you just burn that pick.
  • Round 1 and 2 picks can never be kept. Not by you, not by anyone you trade them to. They are worth nothing before the draft.
  • You can only keep a man once. After his keeper year he must be traded or returned to the pool.
  • Keepability travels. Trade a used–up keeper and his new owner may keep him at his locked cost. This is the engine of the whole market.
  • A player acquired before the keeper deadline burns a keeper year on his new team. Acquired after, he travels with the pick itself.
  • Draft picks are tradeable, current season only.

Waiver adds: the price lock

A waiver pickup costs whatever their average draft position is on the ESPN platform as of 31 July before the draft. That price then locks permanently — it does not reprice each year, and it follows him to new owners. It only resets if he hits the wire again.

That lock is the most under–used thing in this rulebook. A late–summer add who breaks out becomes a cheap contract you can hold, trade, and re–trade for years.

Two rulings worth knowing before you trade

A waiver add priced into Rounds 1 or 2 is keepable — once

If a player you picked up off waivers carried a first– or second–round ADP the next summer, you can still keep him. What happens next is the part that catches people: where a keeper is kept becomes his draft slot for the following year. Keep him at a 1st or 2nd and he is a first– or second–round pick from that moment on, and first and second–round picks can never be kept. So a waiver add who prices into round 1 or 2 buys you exactly one season, and then he is back in the pool.

It has happened ten times since the keeper era began. Only two players have ever been kept at a 1st: Aaron Rodgers (Jon Nairn, 2017) and Alvin Kamara (Joe Nairn, 2018). A 2nd is more common — Doug Baldwin (Eric Counihan, 2017), Dalvin Cook (Brian Haas, 2018), Davante Adams (Brian Clemens, 2018), Jarvis Landry (Ken Tinkham, 2018), Melvin Gordon (Kevin Flanagan, 2018), Justin Jefferson (Ken Tinkham, 2021), Kyren Williams (Kevin Flanagan, 2024), and Bucky Irving (Kevin Flanagan, 2025).

Two keepers at the same cost need two picks at that cost

Keeping is paid for with actual picks. If both of your keepers price out at a 6th, you need two sixth–round picks to declare them both. If you only have one, the second man comes out of your next–highest pick — a 5th — and you choose which of the two is the 5th and which is the 6th.

That choice matters more than it looks, because the round you pay becomes that player's draft slot for next year. Pay the 5th on the man you intend to move on from, and pay the 6th on the one you want to keep cheap in the future. It is also a reason to think twice before trading away a middle–round pick you might need to pay a keeper with.

Quarterbacks are underpriced by our own keeper system. Convert that to value over a replacement–level player at his position. Then compare it to what a pick in that slot returned on average that year.

Beat that number and you are positive. Because the expectation curve is fitted to each draft's own results, the twelve owners in any season sum to zero: you can only gain here at the expense of the other eleven.

What it does not see

This is drafting alone. Waivers, trades and lineup calls — a large share of any season — are invisible to it. Team defences are ungraded because the source data cannot score our D/ST rules, and the forty–yard touchdown bonuses are missing for the same reason.

A player who never took a snap that year scores zero, which is exactly what the pick was worth.

Appendix / Method

How the numbers work

Where every number came from, and how much to trust it.

Some of this guide is a record of what happened. Some of it is an estimate. They are kept separate on purpose, so you know how hard to lean on any given figure. That is the whole machine. One page left.

Measured — taken directly from league records

  • The 16 ESPN–era seasons of standings, records and points (2010–2025), from the final–standings pages. Champions from 2004 forward, from the early league records.
  • All 16 playoff brackets, seeds and scores.
  • Every keeper declared since 2017, and every pick of all sixteen ESPN–era drafts.
  • Weekly player statistics for every season since 2010, scored under our settings.
  • Every 2026 transaction through the keeper deadline.
  • Scoring settings and the rulebook text.

Modelled — estimates, carrying real error

  • The base pick curve. Pick 1.01 anchored at 1,000, decaying to about 45 at pick 180, log–interpolated. There is no published fantasy equivalent of the Jimmy Johnson chart. Any single cell is good to roughly ±15%.
  • The keeper premium. The bump each round gets for being a round that actually produces keepers here. Calibrated on our own history: the chance a pick in that round becomes a keeper, times the gap between the average kept player's value and that round's mid–pick value. It runs from +4 to +29.
  • Projected finish. Each owner’s three inputs — three–year average regular–season finish, 2026 keeper haul, and career draft grade — are standardized against the other eleven and combined at 45% form, 35% haul, 20% draft grade; the twelve totals are then ranked. Rerun after the declarations, so every projection reflects the exact–pick haul.
  • Surplus. Worth at his 2026 ADP slot, minus the value of the pick burned. For the twenty–four declared keepers that is the exact overall pick consumed on 29 August, priced off the same chart as the capital column; for everyone else it is the round’s mid–pick value, since no specific pick was ever burned. Both inputs carry the errors above, so treat ±30 as noise.
  • Expected value by draft slot. Used for the draft and keeper grades. Each season's own results are fitted to a curve of value against pick number, so every draft is graded against the board that league actually produced. Value over replacement uses the twelfth quarterback, thirtieth back, thirty–sixth receiver, twelfth tight end and twelfth kicker in a twelve–team year, and scales with the field when the league was smaller.

Not known — treat these with caution

  • The 2004–2009 standings. We have champions but no win–loss records, so every rate statistic in the record book is an ESPN–era figure.
  • ADP accuracy. Treat any single average–pick figure as approximate. Check it yourself before you trade on it.
  • Who owns which pick. A dozen pick trades have gone through this offseason and the overall numbers logged before the 29 May order change are stale. Check the ESPN draft board. This ledger prices players, not pick inventory.
  • Whether the swap market is finished. Three teams still hold stuck assets — Aura Farmers, Respect My Conglomerate and Stu The Assassin — and the clock runs out on the 29th.
  • The 2019 playoff high points winner. Blank in the rulebook. If you remember, tell the commissioner and we will fill it in.

A note on precision

Do not read these numbers to the point. A surplus of +215 and a surplus of +190 are the same number for practical purposes.
